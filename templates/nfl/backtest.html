How the model works

Each team has an offense rating (points it scores compared with an average team) and a defense rating (points it stops). A team's expected points in a game are the league average, plus its offense, minus the opponent's defense, plus half the home advantage at home (minus half away). Ratings are fitted to the last {{ training_years }} years of games, with each game's weight halving every {{ half_life }} days, and pulled toward average so a few games can't produce extreme ratings.

Quarterback ratings: every starting quarterback has his own rating, in points per game compared with an average starter, fitted alongside the team ratings. Quarterbacks with few career starts are rated lower until they show otherwise. So a backup, a rookie, a new signing or a starter returning from injury each gets an appropriate value.

The expected margin becomes a win probability through the spread of real NFL results: games typically finish about 13 points either side of what was expected. On these held-out seasons, the model's expected margin missed the actual margin by {{ margins.model }} points on average; the market's spread missed by {{ margins.spread }}.

What improved the model

Log loss on the held-out seasons (2,219 games, October 2026). Lower is better; a coin flip scores 0.6931.

Offense and defense ratings alone did no better than simple Elo ratings on the held-out seasons. Quarterbacks made the real difference: first a simple flag for teams not starting their usual quarterback, then individual ratings for every quarterback, which helped most in the first four weeks of a season (when teams have new starters). The model now captures 75% of the market's edge over a coin flip, up from 65% for Elo. Rest days, travel, domes and weather were also tested and didn't improve the forecasts.

The projections beat both benchmarks for every stat. Passing yards improved the most (about 12% smaller misses than the season average), helped by knowing which quarterback is starting and how good the opposing pass defense is. Using the weekly injury reports cut the misses for rushing yards, receptions and receiving yards by another 4–5%, and snap counts by about 1% more. Defense by position and how far downfield a receiver's targets are thrown were also tested and didn't help. The ranges on game pages (where the actual number lands about 80% of the time) held up on these seasons: 79–86% of results fell inside them. There are no free historical betting lines for player stats, so unlike the game forecasts these can't be compared with the market.
